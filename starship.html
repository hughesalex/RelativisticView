What am I looking at?

Aberration. Moving through light is like running through rain: it seems to come at you from further ahead. A star at angle θ from your heading appears at θ′ where cos θ′ = (cos θ + β)/(1 + β cos θ). Everything crowds toward the bow; the sky behind you stretches thin.

Doppler shift. Light from ahead is compressed in frequency by D = γ(1 + β cos θ). A star's blackbody spectrum at temperature T is seen as a blackbody at D·T, so stars ahead look hotter (bluer), stars behind cooler (redder).

Beaming. Each star's total power rises as D² (and diffuse light by D⁴ per unit area), but your eyes only see the visible slice of the spectrum. Ahead, a star first brightens, then, once its peak slides into the ultraviolet, it fades. Behind, everything slides into the infrared and goes dark.

CMB. The 2.7 K microwave background is boosted the same way. Above γ ≈ 100 it starts to glow dull red dead ahead; by γ ≈ 1000 that spot is as hot as the Sun's surface.

Caveats. Positions are always the view from near the Sun (no parallax from travel). Star colours use B−V → blackbody temperature. Nebulae are treated as blackbodies too. Faint stars below magnitude 8 are folded into the Milky Way glow. Data: HYG v3.8 catalogue (Hipparcos/Yale/Gliese), Milky Way and constellation outlines from d3-celestial.
